# Air passengers — Capstone report

## Recommendation

**Ship AutoETS as the working model, subject to the eight-window harness results in the notebook; its advantage is the ability to model both the strong growth and the changing annual seasonal amplitude that the seasonal-naive floor cannot absorb.**

The series contains 144 monthly observations from January 1949 through December 1960 with no gaps. Passenger counts rise from 112 thousand to 432 thousand, while a strong annual pattern repeats and the seasonal swings become larger as the level rises. This shape argues for a model that can represent trend and multiplicative-style seasonality.

## What the series says before modelling

The STL decomposition separates the series into trend, seasonal and remainder components. The trend and seasonal-strength measures are both very high (approximately 1.00 and 0.99 respectively), so the signal is dominated by persistent growth and annual seasonality rather than noise. The raw ACF has a strong lag-12 spike (about 0.76) and a smaller lag-24 spike (about 0.53), which is the signature of year-to-year seasonal dependence.

The seasonal-naive model is therefore a meaningful floor, not a straw man. Its in-sample seasonal-difference scale is about 32 passengers (thousand passenger units), but its residuals are not white noise: the Ljung–Box p-values at 12 and 24 lags are effectively zero. That means repeating last year's month leaves systematic structure on the table, especially the changing level and seasonal amplitude.

## Intervals

The notebook produces 80% and 95% forecast bands for the seasonal-naive floor and 80% bands for the shortlisted model during rolling-origin evaluation. The key test is not whether an interval looks visually wide; it is whether the observed coverage is close to the nominal 80%.

The final verdict should be taken directly from the `RESULTS` table after the notebook is run: a band with materially less than 80% coverage is over-confident and should not be described as honest, while a much wider band with coverage near or above 80% trades precision for reliability. The report should therefore use the notebook's measured `coverage_80` and `scaled_crps`, rather than relying on a single holdout interval.

## Residuals and what they mean

The seasonal-naive residuals are `y_t - y_{t-12}`. Their Ljung–Box results reject the white-noise hypothesis at both 12 and 24 lags, so the floor has not captured all predictable structure. In practical terms, the passenger total is not simply last year's value repeated: the underlying level keeps moving and the size of seasonal peaks changes over time.

For the shortlisted model, the rolling-origin residual evidence should be read alongside MASE, scaled CRPS and coverage. If the model improves MASE but loses on scaled CRPS or coverage, that is a real distributional weakness rather than something to hide behind the point score. The notebook is deliberately structured to report both halves.

## One next step

**The next step should be to test a dynamic regression with one external driver, such as monthly mean temperature for a clearly defined origin region, while keeping the same eight rolling origins and leakage rules.** The driver should be sourced, cached as a CSV, and available only from information that would have been known at each fold. The expected benefit is to test whether an external seasonal signal can improve the predictive distribution rather than merely the point forecast.

## Bottom line

The benchmark establishes a hard standard: any model that cannot beat seasonal naive across the same rolling origins has not earned deployment. AutoETS is the most natural candidate because it matches the observed trend and changing seasonal amplitude, but the **final ship/no-ship decision must be based on the eight-window harness table in the notebook**, not AutoGluon's single internal leaderboard split.
